  • Alcohol Access Shocks and Mortality

    Job Market Paper — Draft available upon request

    A substantial literature links the density of alcohol retail outlets to adverse health and social outcomes, but this evidence is often correlational and confined to a single city or region. In this paper, I provide causal evidence on whether physical alcohol retail access affects mortality. I develop a mortality-risk extension of the rational addiction model with access costs, and test its predictions against reduced-form estimates. I identify the effect from county-level, over-time variation in County Business Patterns liquor store establishment counts, matched to restricted-access NCHS Multiple Cause of Death mortality microdata, across U.S. counties from 1989 to 2016, and estimate two-way fixed effects and event-study specifications. Each additional liquor store per 100,000 residents raises alcohol-primary mortality by 0.048 deaths per 100,000 annually, a 0.65% increase relative to the pre-treatment mean. The effect is three times larger for off-premise retailers (e.g., liquor stores) than for on-premise establishments (e.g., bars), and concentrated among older adults, consistent with a chronic rather than acute consumption channel. Using a standard value of a statistical life, the estimated effect amounts to approximately $556,000 in annual mortality damages per additional store per 100,000 residents.

  • Professional Recommendations and Provider Behavior

    With Lauren Hoehn-Velasco, Elizabeth Humphreys, and Nancy A. Niemczyk

    We examine how physicians respond to new RCT evidence, using the publication of the ARRIVE trial as a natural experiment. The trial found that elective induction at 39 weeks reduces cesarean delivery in low-risk, first-time pregnancies. Using nationally representative U.S. birth certificate data from 2016–2021, we document a rapid and sustained 9% increase in 39-week inductions for low-risk first births following the trial's release. Inductions also rise among subsequent births, suggesting off-label application of the findings. Despite this significant shift in clinical practice, we find no corresponding decline in cesarean sections. These results highlight a discrepancy between trial-based evidence and real-world outcomes, challenging the assumption that RCT findings necessarily translate into population-level improvements.

  • The Long-term Impact of In-Utero Alcohol Taxes on Adult Prenatal Drinking

    With Godwin Aipoh

    Prior research documents the effects of alcohol taxes on drinking and birth outcomes, but their long-term intergenerational effects remain largely unexplored. We investigate whether in-utero exposure to state alcohol taxes shapes adult prenatal drinking decades later. Using U.S. birth certificate data (1989–2004) on 11.7 million first births, we link each mother's state and year of birth to the alcohol tax her mother faced during pregnancy, and estimate models with state and year fixed effects and cohort trends; event studies show no pre-trends. A one-dollar increase in the real in-utero alcohol tax per gallon of ethanol lowers the probability of prenatal drinking by 0.07 percentage points (about 7 percent of the mean). The effect operates entirely on the extensive margin, is concentrated among older, more-educated mothers, and comes with suggestive improvements in infant health, highlighting the durability of early-life policy interventions.

Short Bio

Raka Datta is a PhD Candidate in Economics at the Andrew Young School of Policy Studies, Georgia State University, advised by Professor Lauren Hoehn-Velasco. Her research applies causal inference tools on large administrative and panel data to study questions in health and labor economics using applied microeconometric methods. She focuses on alcohol policy, provider behavior, and maternal and child health outcomes.

Prior to her PhD, she received an M.Sc. in Economics from the University of Bonn and worked as a Research Assistant at IZA - Institute of Labor Economics in Bonn, in the program Gender, Growth and Labor Markets in Low Income Countries.
